## Understanding stakeholders and their stakes

- **Two kinds of power:** formal power is the title, the budget and the sign-off; informal power is whose opinion the decision-maker checks first, and who can stall a decision without ever saying no. Read both before asking for anything. Missed: the hardest no in Module 2 went to the CEO (the therapist-matching tier), and the course routes this module's message to Growth instead. The CEO's no is still unsaid, and the CEO holds both kinds of power.

| | Resistant | Aligned |
|---|---|---|
| **High influence** | Blockers: find what they are protecting and address that | Sponsors: give them the narrative and a specific ask, so they can make the case without you |
| **Low influence** | Noise to manage: acknowledge and answer briefly, without letting them set the agenda | Validators: tell senior sponsors they're on board |

- **The map is for moves, not filing:** each quadrant calls for a different move. Missed: Growth could have been a blocker, since the bedtime reminder needs lifecycle messaging they own. Making them co-owner in Module 2 moved them towards partner before this message was written, which is the map used rather than drawn.

| Resistance | What it is | What moves them |
|---|---|---|
| Logical | They don't believe the argument | Better evidence, clearer reasoning or a proof point |
| Interest | The proposal costs them something real | Find where interests meet, and show how the ask helps their goal |
| Identity | The proposal challenges a decision they're known for | Frame the ask as the next step, building on what they did |

- **Name the type before choosing the move:** Growth's resistance is interest and identity, so the message answers both: the referral invite meets their interest, and "you pushed for social features and streaks to keep people longer" honours the bet they're known for. Missed: logical resistance is the one this message barely touches, because the evidence that the steady plan works doesn't exist until the concierge test reports.
- **The five whys:** ask why five times, from the stated objection down to a root cause you can fix; in the course's example, the root cause was assuming we already knew what the CPO cared about. Missed: the AI check did the same job for the message. It found Growth's real problem, unchanged targets, under the stated one, the lost features.

## Making and defending hard calls

- **Three hard calls with no clean answer:** whose work gets cut, when to overrule a team, and backing a decision you were overruled on as your own. Missed: Fable has all three. Growth's and the clinical team's bets were cut; the charter lets the product lead overrule a PM; and if the CPO sides with Growth on targets, the product lead has to back that as their own.
- **Three costs instinct can miss:** the organisation (every yes sets a precedent and shows what gets rewarded), relationships (a fair call still costs a peer's trust if they weren't brought in) and the business (what it does to revenue, speed or risk later). Missed: the relationship cost is why the buddy test is co-designed with Growth rather than handed over when it starts.
- **After the call:** the team sees your conviction, rarely your doubts; seniority puts you further from each decision's detail; the calls that need most thought often get least time; and the weight of the decision stays with you, however many opinions arrive. Missed: a decision log, like the one the charter requires, is how a leader looks back at calls honestly rather than from memory.
- **Saying no so it holds:** start with what stays open, say it plainly, be firm on the call and warm with the person, and offer the path forward in the same message. Test: could they repeat it back as a clear no? Missed: this order conflicts with the module's other rule, bottom line up front. Claude read the opening of the first draft as softening Growth up. For a peer the course's order holds; to an executive, the no goes first.

## Building alignment through negotiation

- **The executive narrative:** the recommendation (plain enough to repeat without you), the trade-off (named by you, which shows you've weighed it) and the reason to trust it (evidence, precedent or an early signal). Delivered as a one-page pre-read that makes the case without you, or a three-minute spoken ask that opens with the recommendation and answers the question you know is coming. Missed: Module 5's business case is Fable's pre-read for the board, so its first line should be the ask: fund the concierge test, with its stop rule.
- **Bottom line up front:** an executive may read only the first line, so the ask goes there, with the cost and the cost of not doing it straight after. Missed: the course's example names a dollar cost and an expected question; Fable's board pre-read needs both, which is Module 5's job.

| | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Position | What you ask for | "I need two of your engineers for six weeks" |
| Interest | Why you need it | "We both need checkout stable before the holiday peak" |
| BATNA | What you'll do if you can't agree | "I'll take one engineer for three weeks and move the redesign" |

- **Negotiate from interests:** two people with clashing positions often share an interest, and that's where a deal is. Four steps: come prepared (what you need versus what you ask for, and your BATNA); open by observing, then ask how and what questions; name the real disagreement, which is often not the one being argued; and when stuck, go back to interests and ask for options neither side has suggested. Missed: the Fable negotiation pits the core product leader against the Growth product leader over the same engineers, the tension of this module's message. Their shared interest is retention past 90 days, which GPT-6's check named as Growth's own goal.
