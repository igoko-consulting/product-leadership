## GPT-6

The strategy is clear and commercially coherent. I can identify exactly where Fable will play: post-acute, premium users aged 25–45, particularly those still using sleep content as their original need eases. The winning proposition is also distinctive: use what Fable already knows helped each individual to create a nightly wind-down they actively choose to keep. The refusal to build community, social sharing or streaks is convincing, particularly given the vulnerability of the audience. The strongest strategic insight is recognising that some churn is healthy. That gives the strategy genuine conviction rather than simply disguising engagement growth as customer value. The wish-list risk sits mainly in Later: buddy experiments, freemium upgrades and employer/insurer propositions are three quite different growth directions. They are appropriately gated, but should remain explicitly speculative rather than appear as an emerging roadmap.

The roadmap, charter, stakeholder message and kill criterion largely follow the strategy. The sequencing is sensible: concierge validation before automation, regulatory clearance before self-checks, and evidence before AI personalisation. The charter establishes meaningful decision rights, particularly around clinical safety and Growth collaboration. The stakeholder message is particularly strong: it acknowledges Growth’s commercial objectives, maintains the hard no and offers an alternative referral experiment without compromising privacy. The roadmap is the weakest element. Bet 1 has excellent validation criteria, but Bets 2 and 3 are less rigorously specified: Bet 3 lacks a quantified success threshold, and Bet 2 needs a clearer measurement definition. There is also a significant financial question: the $155 lifetime margin per retained customer appears inconsistent with $8.40 monthly margin and 6% monthly churn over 18 months. That calculation needs reconciling before the business case is presented.

My one recommended change: strengthen the evidence chain connecting the concierge experiment to the $7.4M opportunity. The presentation moves convincingly from strategic hypothesis to test, but the financial return depends on assumptions that deserve more scrutiny. In particular, distinguish the retention lift observed in the small, consenting concierge group from the lift realistically achievable across the full monthly premium cohort. Show how that translates into incremental retained subscribers, actual contribution margin after churn, and payback. This is the difference between a compelling product strategy and a board-ready investment case. The strategic thinking is already there; the final step is making the economics equally defensible.

Needs another pass — not because the strategy is weak, but because the financial assumptions and two roadmap success measures need tightening before submission.

1. Strategy clarity. The strategy is clear. You play with adults who used Fable heavily through a hard stretch and are now at day 60 to 120, reached in the app, premium users first. You win by using what each person's history shows helped them, built into a nightly wind-down. You refuse social features, competing with Headspace and Calm for new users, selling to employers, and clinical care. The refusals are the strongest part, because each one comes with a reason tied to the people you serve. Two things still read like a wish list:

* The Later horizon has five bets, one of which is the employer offer the strategy rules out. Gating them doesn't stop it looking like a parking lot. Cut it to the two that follow from the moat: the acute-level evidence and the fast route back.
* The age band (25 to 45) never shows up again in the KRs, the bets or the business case. Either use it or drop it.

2. Do the parts follow from the strategy? Mostly, yes:

* Roadmap: it is sequenced around proving demand before building.
* Charter: it makes PM 2 accountable for KR1 to KR3 and keeps a clinical veto.
* Stakeholder message: it leads with the risk to users rather than capacity, as your Insights say it should, and gives Growth something real (a referral invite that shares nothing about anyone's use).

One label works against you. Rock 1 is sourced from backlog #6, "AI-generated daily check-in prompts". That contradicts "Fable never starts a check-in", the deferral of AI personalisation and the streak hard no. Relabel it as a reframe so a board member doesn't stop on it.

The kill criterion is the weakest part. Its arithmetic doesn't hold:

* Sample size: 30% of 400 invited is 120 participants, so the test can't also have "at least 150 churned participants". Even if it did, 10% of 150 is only 15 people resuming payment, which is too few to decide anything.
* Timing: KR1 is set for month 12, but the volume target counts cohorts reaching day 90 by month 12. Those cohorts don't reach day 180 until about month 15.
* Lifetime margin: at 6% monthly churn the expected lifetime is about 17 months, not 18. That puts each retained user at roughly $140, not $155, and the yearly total nearer $6.7M than $7.4M.

None of this breaks the case, but a stop rule only works if nobody can argue with its numbers.

3. The one change. Rebuild the concierge test's numbers so they hold together:

* Separate groups: set invite and participant minimums separately for churned and current users (for example, at least 500 churned invited to get 150 churned participants).
* Holdout lift: state the lift the test needs over the matched holdout, not just an absolute threshold.
* One timeline: make KR1, the volume target and the second kill line all use the same cohort dates, and recompute the per-user margin from the churn rate.

Needs another pass: the strategy is strong, but the kill criterion, which your own Insights call the core of the bet, can't be met as written. It's a quick fix.
