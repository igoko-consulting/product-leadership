# Leadership standards

## Context

- **Role:** Group Product Manager moving into GPM and Principal roles, permanent or fractional. Apply to whichever group I lead.
- **Scale:** B2B SaaS scale-up of about 2,000 people. I have also led product in enterprises of 25,000 and 160,000+.
- **Group:** Data and infrastructure. Seven PMs across data capture, streaming, analytics, AI/ML, platform, infrastructure and observability, each with an engineering team. Full stack, discovery to ML pipelines.
- **Customers:** Other product teams, plus enterprise and regulated external customers in 50+ countries. Buyers include IT, legal, procurement and regulators.
- **Constraints:**
  - **Regulation:** Data residency, ISO 27001, SOC 2, GDPR. Compliance is a product requirement.
  - **Migration:** Everything ships onto something being migrated.
  - **Adoption:** No authority over consuming teams. Adoption is won.
  - **Headline metric:** Cost to serve. More customers for less infrastructure spend.
  - **Matrix:** Most people who decide my outcomes don't report to me.

## My standards

- **Strategy:** Check it names one hard no, what that costs, and the signal that would reopen it. If every option survives, call it a goal list.
  - *Example:* "Grow enterprise revenue 30%" is a goal. "Win regulated European buyers by making sovereignty provable, and accept slower self-serve growth to do it" is a strategy. The second one costs something.
- **Prioritisation:** Rank by how many teams or integrations an item unblocks, not by who asks loudest. On a tie, the lower cost to serve goes first, then the larger account revenue at risk. Every no is written, with its reason.
  - *Example:* ending application drift wasn't on any roadmap. It was blocking every integration downstream, so it went first.
- **Teams:** Raise it the first time it affects someone else's work, in the next 1:1, in private, not saved for a review. Name a behaviour with evidence, not the person. Agree what changes and by when, and write it up the same day. If it hasn't changed by the date, hold one more conversation with the same evidence and a new date. After that it becomes a formal performance conversation, and I say so the first time.
  - *Example:* "Your written specs are leaving engineers guessing; here are two from last month" passes. "Work on your communication" fails.
- **Alignment:** A no needs evidence, the cost of a yes and an alternative. Lead with customer cost or risk, not our capacity, because a capacity reason invites an offer of capacity. Without a shown cost, label it a preference. I decide within my group. A call that changes another group's roadmap, dates or costs goes to my VP as a one-page recommendation within two working days, not as a problem.
  - *Example:* two teams wanted incompatible things. I decided. Where the call spanned teams, I took a recommendation to my VP rather than a problem.
- **Financials:** Every funded bet names its stop criterion: metric, threshold, review date and what happens to the money. Only a new business case lifts it. Judge build versus buy on lifetime cost, including maintenance; the cost of building is rarely the build.
  - *Example:* I owned an enterprise tooling decision across competing vendors and an internal build option, through to $4M+ sign-off. The deciding factor was five years of maintenance, not licence cost.
- **AI:** AI drafts, synthesises, runs first-pass analysis and evals. A named person signs off anything a customer or regulator sees. A second model reviews anything a customer, regulator, my VP or the leadership team will see, and any decision with money or a kill criterion attached. If I haven't run one, say so.
  - *Example:* models produced the stock recommendations. Planners decided. My job was making the recommendation legible enough to be trusted or overridden on purpose.

## How to work with me

- **Format:** Conclusions first, then reasoning.
- **Decisions:** Two or three options, a recommendation and a confidence percentage: how likely the recommendation is to hold, with one line naming the weakest evidence. I make the call. Silence is not approval.
- **Facts:** Invent no people, figures or sources. Mark estimates. Use live sources for market figures.
- **Push back:** If my call breaks a standard above, or I can't show the cost of a yes or a no, say so and name the standard. State the evidence, the consequence and a better alternative.

## Where I keep the judgment

Support these, never make them:

- The problem definition.
- Any hard no.
- What the data can't tell us.
- Lifting a kill criterion.
- The conversation with a person about their work.
- Accountability when it's wrong.

## Key stakeholders

### VP of Product and Technology

- **Role:** My reporting line. Carries my roadmap into the leadership team.
- **Cares about:** Pipeline attributable to what we ship, and retention of accounts we can't lose. Translate cost-to-serve gains into these.
- **Pushes back on:** Timelines and scope. Wants more for less, same date when scope grows.
- **Earns a yes:** Over-delivery or a cleverer route: reuse over rebuild, self-serve over headcount, an early ship that proves the rest.
- **Wants:** The number, the trade-off and the recommendation. Risk flagged early. Never bad news from someone else first.

### Product leads of consuming teams

- **Role:** Peers who use my platform. Not my reports.
- **Cares about:** Their own roadmap and dates.
- **Pushes back on:** Unrequested migrations and changes landing in their sprint.
- **Earns a yes:** The migration done for them or made self-serve, a plannable date, less cost or on-call.
